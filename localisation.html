Vehicle Mechatronics · Localisation

Where am I, exactly?

An automated vehicle fuses many answers to that question: satellites overhead, an inertial sensor that feels every motion, the wheels, and the shape of the world around it. Play with each one — then watch a real ICP solver localise a LiDAR scan against a map.

Inertial Measurement Unit

Feeling every motion — 6 degrees of freedom

An IMU measures acceleration along 3 axes and angular velocity about 3 axes. From a flat rest it feels only gravity pointing "up" through its z-axis; tilt it and gravity spreads into x and y. Tumble the chip and watch all six numbers, then see why the errors drift.

Why fuse with GNSS?

Integrating noisy inertial data lets small errors compound — position drift grows ≈ t3/2.

Wheel odometry — the other dead-reckoning sensor

From wheel rotation: speed v = r·ω. Cheap and always available, but wheel slip and accumulating error make it a fusion input, not a standalone truth.
Like the IMU, odometry drifts — it's great over seconds, unreliable over minutes. That's the recurring theme of localisation: every sensor is wrong in its own way, so vehicles fuse them (e.g. an Extended Kalman Filter blending GNSS + IMU + odometry).
Simultaneous Localisation & Mapping

Matching a LiDAR scan to the map

GNSS is often unreliable (tunnels, cities), so vehicles localise against the world itself. Each LiDAR scan is a point cloud; SLAM finds the pose (X, Y, θ) that best aligns the scan to the known map — point-cloud registration. The classic method is Iterative Closest Point (ICP). This one is real — it runs the actual algorithm.

How each iteration works

"Limited math" version — the same steps the slides derive with SVD (Kabsch algorithm).
Match: for every scan point, find the closest map point. (Correspondences are unknown, so we guess them each round.)
Centre: subtract each cloud's centroid, so only rotation remains: s̃ = s − s̄, m̃ = m − m̄.
Solve rotation R and translation t that minimise Σ‖m − R·s − t‖² (closed-form via SVD of the cross-covariance).
Apply & repeat: move the scan by (R, t). Re-match and iterate until it stops improving.
minimise  Σ ‖ mᵢ − R·sᵢ − t ‖²
  H = Σ s̃ᵢ m̃ᵢᵀ  →  U Σ Vᵀ = svd(H)
  R = V·diag(1, det(VUᵀ))·Uᵀ    t = m̄ − R·s̄

Sensor fusion · Kalman filter

Better than any one sensor

Here's the payoff, and where the ICP you just built comes back. A Kalman filter fuses three flawed sources: dead-reckoning (IMU + odometry) — smooth but drifting; GNSS — unbiased but noisy and lost in tunnels; and LiDAR + ICP scan-matching — accurate wherever there's structure to match. Each step it predicts with the motion, then corrects with whatever measurements are available. Drive into the tunnel: GNSS drops, but ICP keeps the fix locked.

Visual SLAM

Localising from a plain camera

Cameras are cheap and don't need depth — a stream of 2D images is enough. VSLAM detects features in each frame, matches them to the next frame, and recovers how the camera moved. The catch: a single camera can't recover scale — how far, only which direction — so IMU or stereo is fused back in.

Step 1 — Feature detection

A detector slides a small kernel over the image and fires where the texture is distinctive — corners, blobs. This is the intuition behind SIFT / SURF / ORB (and learned CNN detectors).

Step 2 — Match & estimate motion

The same features are found in the next frame; matching them reveals the camera's rotation and translation (up to scale) — the 8-point algorithm.

The VSLAM pipeline

From a stream of images to a 3D map and a trajectory.
Detect features→ Match across frames→ Estimate R, t (up to scale)→ Triangulate to 3D points→ Bundle adjustment→ Loop closure (graph SLAM)
ORB-SLAMThe widely-used classical VSLAM system — real-time features, tracking, mapping and loop closure.
Scale problemMonocular VSLAM gets direction but not metric distance; fuse IMU or use a stereo pair to recover real-world scale.
Deep VSLAMLearned systems like DROID-SLAM now reach excellent accuracy — though LiDAR SLAM is still generally more robust.
