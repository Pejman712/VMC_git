Vehicle Mechatronics · Novel View Synthesis

Painting the world with millions of blurry dots

3D Gaussian Splatting turns a set of photos into a scene you can fly through in real time — not with a neural network you query, but with millions of little translucent 3D ellipsoids you simply draw. This page builds the idea up piece by piece, interactively, then surveys where the field went next.

The big idea

An explicit scene you can draw, not a network you query

In 2020, NeRF[2] showed you could store a whole 3D scene inside a neural network and render photorealistic new views — but rendering meant firing hundreds of network queries down every pixel's ray, so a single frame took seconds. In 2023, Kerbl et al.[1] asked a different question: what if the scene were made of explicit primitives we could rasterise directly? Their answer — a cloud of 3D Gaussians — hit photorealistic quality at ≥100 FPS, and reset the field.

Both approaches optimise a radiance field (what colour every point emits, in every direction) so that rendering it reproduces your input photos. The difference is the representation — and that difference is why splatting is fast enough for robotics, VR, and live mapping.

NeRF vs. 3D Gaussian Splatting

Same goal — reproduce the photos from new viewpoints — very different machinery.

The whole method in one breath

Start from a sparse point cloud that Structure-from-Motion already gives you for free while estimating the camera poses.
Put a Gaussian at each point — a soft, coloured, semi-transparent ellipsoid.
Render by projecting every Gaussian into the image and blending them (differentiably).
Compare to the real photo, backpropagate the error, nudge every Gaussian's shape and colour.
Grow / prune the set where detail is missing or wasted — then repeat.
The rest of this page is just these five steps, slowed down and made interactive. Everything below runs live in your browser.
Step 1 · Anatomy of a Gaussian

What exactly is one splat?

A single 3D Gaussian is a fuzzy ellipsoid described by just a handful of numbers. Drag the sliders to reshape the one below and watch its covariance matrix — the object that encodes its size and orientation — update live. This is a 2D slice; the real thing is the same idea in 3D.

The five attributes stored per Gaussian

That's all a splat is — no neural network hiding inside.

Where it sits in 3D — its mean (x, y, z). 3 numbers.
ΣCovariance
Its size & orientation, stored as scale s (3) + rotation quaternion q (4) so Σ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ stays valid.

How much light it blocks / contributes, 0–1. 1 number.

View-dependent colour via spherical harmonics — so highlights shift as you move. up to 48 numbers.

Why store scale + rotation instead of Σ directly? Gradient descent could push a raw Σ to something invalid (not a real ellipse). Factoring it as R S SᵀRᵀ guarantees a positive-definite covariance at every step.[1]

Why "spherical harmonics" for colour?

A wet road, a glossy car, a metal rail — their colour changes with your viewing angle. A flat RGB can't do that; SH can.

Each Gaussian stores a small set of SH coefficients per colour channel. Evaluated in the direction from the Gaussian to your eye, they reconstruct a smoothly direction-varying colour. Degree 0 is a constant (plain RGB); adding degrees 1–3 lets the splat encode specular highlights and subtle sheen.

colour toward camera Acolour toward camera B
Turning off SH (degree 0 only) is a common compression trick[12][14] — it slashes memory at a small quality cost.
Step 2 · The splatting rasteriser

From 3D ellipsoids to pixels — and why order matters

"Splatting" means: project each 3D Gaussian onto the image (it becomes a 2D Gaussian "footprint"), then blend the footprints together. Blending is order-dependent — a near, opaque splat must cover a far one behind it. Get the order wrong and the image falls apart. Toggle the sort below to see it.

Alpha compositing, live

Five overlapping splats drifting in depth. With correct front-to-back sorting the nearer ones occlude; without it, far splats bleed through.

How each pixel gets its colour

The exact blend the rasteriser runs, per pixel, over the splats that cover it (sorted near→far).
C = Σᵢ  cᵢ αᵢ  Πⱼ<ᵢ (1 − αⱼ)

running transmittance  T ← T · (1 − αᵢ)
Project: the 3D covariance Σ becomes a 2D one via the camera Jacobian (the "EWA" splatting trick[3]) — each ellipsoid casts an elliptical footprint.
Sort: order the splats by depth. 3DGS does this globally per frame with a fast GPU radix sort.
Tile: split the screen into 16×16 tiles; each tile only blends the splats that touch it — this is what makes it real-time.
Blend: walk front→back, accumulating colour weighted by opacity and remaining transmittance T, until T is nearly 0.
The blend is a weighted sum of smooth functions — so it's differentiable. That single fact is what lets the whole scene be learned by gradient descent, which is Step 3.
Step 3 · The training loop

Photos in, gradients out

Because rendering is differentiable, training is a plain optimisation loop: render a view we have a photo of, measure how wrong it looks, and let the error flow back to every Gaussian's position, shape, colour and opacity. Step through the cycle below and watch a toy scene sharpen from a sparse point cloud into a faithful render.

One optimisation cycle

Left: current render. Right: the target photo. They converge as the loop runs.
Render→ Compare (loss)→ Backprop→ Adam step→ Densify
iter 0 · splats 0

What each stage does

Render the current Gaussians from this camera into an image.
Compare to the ground-truth photo with a loss that mixes pixel error and structural similarity: L = (1−λ)·L₁ + λ·L_D-SSIM.
Backpropagate that loss through the differentiable rasteriser to get a gradient for every attribute of every splat.
Update all attributes with the Adam optimiser (a small, adaptive gradient step).
Densify & prune every ~100 iters — the subject of Step 4.

This mirrors the real recipe from Kerbl et al.[1] — a rendered image is the supervision signal; no 3D ground truth is needed, only the photos you already took.
Step 4 · Adaptive density control

Spending Gaussians where they're needed

A fixed set of splats can't be right everywhere: blank walls waste them, fine detail starves. So during training the optimiser edits the set itself — cloning splats into under-reconstructed areas, splitting over-large ones, and deleting near-transparent ones. This is the secret behind the sharpness, and it's driven entirely by the gradient signal.

Clone

Under-reconstruction: detail is missing.

A small Gaussian in a region with large positional gradient is copied and nudged along the gradient — adding coverage where the render is still blurry.

Split

Over-reconstruction: one blob does too much.

A large Gaussian with high gradient is replaced by two smaller ones (scaled down by ~1.6×), sampled from its own distribution — trading one coarse splat for finer ones.

Prune

Waste: it barely contributes.

Any Gaussian whose opacity falls below a threshold (α < 0.005) is deleted. Opacity is also periodically reset toward zero so dead splats get cleaned up.

The rule in one line

if  ‖∇μ L‖ > τ:  clone (if small)  or  split (if large)    |    if  α < ε:  prune

A scene often starts at ~100k splats from SfM and grows to 1–5 million. Managing that budget is an active research theme on its own — treating it as a principled sampling process[11], or capping and compressing the count for the same quality[10][12][14].

The field since 2023

Why robotics cares (the VMC angle)

For a vehicle, a splat map is attractive for concrete reasons:

Real-timeRendering fast enough to sit inside a perception or simulation loop.
Dense & metricA continuous photometric map, not just sparse feature points — and it doubles as a SLAM map.[29][30][31]
Sim-readyPhotoreal reconstructions become sensor-in-the-loop simulators for training & testing.
EditableObjects are explicit primitives — remove a parked car, add an obstacle, relight the scene.[33]
This is the same story as the Localisation page: LiDAR & visual SLAM build a map to localise against — Gaussian Splatting is a new, photorealistic kind of that map.

Major research directions

Each card points to representative papers; full citations are in the reference list.

▦

Anti-aliasing & quality

Plain 3DGS shimmers and blurs when you zoom or change resolution. Mip-Splatting adds a 3D smoothing + 2D mip filter to render alias-free at any scale; MCMC-based optimisation improves robustness and count control.

Papers: [6] Mip-Splatting · [11] 3DGS-MCMC
◭

Geometry & surfaces

Fuzzy blobs make lovely images but noisy geometry. 2D Gaussian Splatting, SuGaR and Gaussian Opacity Fields flatten or regularise the splats so you can extract clean, watertight meshes.

Papers: [7] 2DGS · [8] GOF · [9] SuGaR
⇩

Compression & efficiency

Millions of splats are heavy. Scaffold-GS, LightGaussian, compact-representation and vector-quantisation methods cut storage by 10–30× — from gigabytes to tens of megabytes — with little quality loss.

Papers: [10] Scaffold-GS · [12] LightGaussian · [13] Compressed-3DGS · [14] Compact-3DGS · [15] EAGLES · [16] SOG
◱

Large scenes & LOD

City blocks and drone captures overflow GPU memory. Hierarchical 3DGS, Octree-GS and VastGaussian add level-of-detail and partitioning to render vast datasets in real time.

Papers: [17] Hierarchical-3DGS · [18] Octree-GS · [19] VastGaussian
◷

Dynamic & 4D scenes

The world moves. 4D-GS, Dynamic 3D Gaussians, Deformable-3DGS and Spacetime Gaussians add time — deforming splats to capture and replay moving scenes and even track points.

Papers: [20] 4D-GS · [21] Dynamic-3DG · [22] Deformable-3DGS · [23] Spacetime-GS
✦

Generation (text/image → 3D)

Marry splatting with 2D diffusion priors and you get fast 3D asset creation. DreamGaussian makes a textured mesh in minutes; GaussianDreamer and GSGEN push text-to-3D quality.

Papers: [24] DreamGaussian · [25] GaussianDreamer · [26] GSGEN
⧉

Feed-forward reconstruction

Skip per-scene optimisation entirely: a network predicts the Gaussians in one forward pass. pixelSplat and MVSplat reconstruct from just a couple of images, generalising across scenes.

Papers: [27] pixelSplat · [28] MVSplat
◎

SLAM & robotics

Build the splat map and localise at once. SplaTAM, Gaussian-Splatting SLAM and GS-SLAM do dense RGB-D or monocular tracking-and-mapping with a Gaussian map.

Papers: [29] SplaTAM · [30] MonoGS · [31] GS-SLAM
☺

Avatars & physics

Rig the splats to a body or drive them with a simulator. GaussianAvatars makes controllable photoreal heads; PhysGaussian runs continuum mechanics directly on the Gaussians.

Papers: [32] GaussianAvatars · [33] PhysGaussian
◐

Foundations & lineage

Splatting didn't appear from nowhere: differentiable point-based rendering (Pulsar, DSS) and classic EWA splatting set the stage that 3DGS built on.

Papers: [3] EWA · [4] Pulsar · [5] DSS
